## 2. Traffic Engineering and Arterial Mobility

### 2.1 The Fundamental Traffic Flow Equation and Greenshields Model

#### Mathematical Concept and Physical Derivation:
In transportation fluid mechanics (Lighthill-Whitham-Richards continuum theory), traffic along an uninterrupted roadway behaves analogously to a compressible one-dimensional fluid. 

The conservation of vehicles along a highway segment of differential length $dx$ over differential time $dt$ is governed by the continuity partial differential equation:

$$\frac{\partial k}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial q}{\partial x} = 0$$

Where:
- $k(x, t)$ is the vehicle concentration or spatial density (vehicles per kilometer).
- $q(x, t)$ is the vehicular throughput flow rate (vehicles per hour).

The fundamental hydrodynamic relation connecting flow, density, and space-mean speed is:

$$q = k \cdot v$$

In 1935, Dr. Bruce Greenshields formulated the classic empirical model of traffic flow, observing that under single-regime conditions, space-mean velocity decreases linearly as spatial vehicle density increases:

$$v(k) = v_f \left(1 - \frac{k}{k_{\text{jam}}}\right)$$

Substituting this linear speed-density relationship into the fundamental flow equation yields a downward-opening parabolic flow-density curve:

$$q(k) = k \cdot v(k) = k \cdot v_f \left(1 - \frac{k}{k_{\text{jam}}}\right) = v_f \left(k - \frac{k^2}{k_{\text{jam}}}\right)$$

To determine the critical density ($k_{\text{crit}}$) at which the roadway delivers its absolute maximum vehicle throughput (road capacity $q_{\max}$), we compute the first derivative of flow with respect to density and set it to zero:

$$\frac{dq}{dk} = v_f \left(1 - \frac{2k}{k_{\text{jam}}}\right) = 0$$

$$1 - \frac{2k}{k_{\text{jam}}} = 0 \implies k_{\text{crit}} = \frac{k_{\text{jam}}}{2}$$

Substituting critical density $k_{\text{crit}}$ back into the parabolic flow equation yields the theoretical maximum roadway capacity:

$$q_{\max} = v_f \left(\frac{k_{\text{jam}}}{2} - \frac{(k_{\text{jam}} / 2)^2}{k_{\text{jam}}}\right) = v_f \left(\frac{k_{\text{jam}}}{2} - \frac{k_{\text{jam}}}{4}\right)$$

$$q_{\max} = \frac{v_f \cdot k_{\text{jam}}}{4}$$

At maximum capacity, traffic moves at exactly half of the free-flow speed:

$$v_{\text{crit}} = v(k_{\text{crit}}) = v_f \left(1 - \frac{k_{\text{jam}} / 2}{k_{\text{jam}}}\right) = \frac{v_f}{2}$$

#### Variable Definitions, Units, and Normal Ranges:
- `q`: Traffic Flow or Volume. The number of vehicles passing a sensor station per hour (unit: veh/h per lane, normal range: `400` to `2400 veh/h`).
- `k`: Traffic Density. The concentration of vehicles packing the roadway (unit: veh/km per lane, normal range: `10` empty road to `140 veh/km` standstill).
- `v`: Space-Mean Speed. The harmonic average velocity of vehicles traversing the segment (unit: km/h, normal range: `10` crawl to `50 km/h` arterial limit).
- `v_f`: Free-Flow Speed. The speed of traffic when density approaches zero (calibrated to `60.0 km/h` on arterial corridors).
- `k_jam`: Jam Density. The bumper-to-bumper standstill density where speed drops to zero (calibrated to `140.0 veh/km` per lane).
- `q_max`: Maximum Road Capacity. The peak sustainable throughput (calibrated to `2100` to `2400 veh/h` per lane on a 3-lane arterial).

#### Worked Numerical Example:
On a 3-lane arterial corridor (`v_f = 60.0 km/h`, `k_jam = 140.0 veh/km` per lane):
1. **Scenario A: Light Morning Traffic (`k = 28.0 veh/km` per lane):**
   - Speed calculation:
     $$v = 60.0 \cdot \left(1 - \frac{28.0}{140.0}\right) = 60.0 \cdot (1 - 0.20) = 60.0 \cdot 0.80 = 48.0\text{ km/h}$$
   - Flow calculation:
     $$q = k \cdot v = 28.0 \cdot 48.0 = 1344\text{ vehicles/hour per lane}$$
   - Total 3-lane corridor flow:
     $$q_{\text{corridor}} = 3 \cdot 1344 = 4032\text{ veh/h}$$
   - Engineering interpretation: High speed, low density. Commuters travel near the legal speed limit without delay (LOS A).
2. **Scenario B: Severe Evening Congestion (`k = 105.0 veh/km` per lane):**
   - Speed calculation:
     $$v = 60.0 \cdot \left(1 - \frac{105.0}{140.0}\right) = 60.0 \cdot (1 - 0.75) = 60.0 \cdot 0.25 = 15.0\text{ km/h}$$
   - Flow calculation:
     $$q = k \cdot v = 105.0 \cdot 15.0 = 1575\text{ vehicles/hour per lane}$$
   - Engineering interpretation: Vehicle density has exceeded critical density ($k_{\text{crit}} = 70.0$). Traffic has entered the oversaturated, unstable regime. Queue shockwaves propagate upstream, and Level of Service collapses to LOS F.

### 2.2 Level of Service (LOS): Indo-HCM (IRC:106) vs US HCM Standards

Level of Service is the standardized qualitative measure describing operational traffic conditions and commuter perceived delay.

| LOS Grade | Operational Condition | Driver Experience | Speed Range (km/h) | Volume/Capacity (V/C) | Delay per Vehicle (s) |
| :---: | :--- | :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **LOS A** | Free Flow | Completely unimpeded; drivers choose operating speed. | > 42.0 | < 0.35 | < 10.0 |
| **LOS B** | Reasonably Free Flow | Slight restriction on lane maneuvers; platoon formation. | 38.0 to 42.0 | 0.35 to 0.50 | 10.0 to 20.0 |
| **LOS C** | Stable Flow | Freedom to maneuver noticeably restricted by presence of others. | 32.0 to 38.0 | 0.50 to 0.65 | 20.0 to 35.0 |
| **LOS D** | Approaching Unstable | High density; speed fluctuations; small incidents cause shockwaves. | 25.0 to 32.0 | 0.65 to 0.80 | 35.0 to 55.0 |
| **LOS E** | Unstable Flow | Operating at maximum roadway capacity; stop-and-go waves. | 18.0 to 25.0 | 0.80 to 1.00 | 55.0 to 80.0 |
| **LOS F** | Forced / Breakdown | Severe congestion; breakdown of flow; queues spill upstream. | < 18.0 | > 1.00 (Oversaturated) | > 80.0 |

#### The Indian Urban Context: Why Indo-HCM (IRC:106) Matters:
Standard Western traffic guidelines (US Highway Capacity Manual, HCM 6th Edition) assume disciplined, homogeneous lane discipline dominated by private passenger cars. 

In Indian municipal arterials, traffic is highly heterogeneous, comprising a dynamic modal mix:
- Powered Two-Wheelers (motorcycles, scooters): 45% to 55% of vehicle count.
- Three-Wheelers (auto-rickshaws): 15% to 20%.
- Standard Passenger Cars: 20% to 30%.
- Heavy Commercial Vehicles & City Buses: 5% to 10%.

To account for differing spatial footprints and maneuverability, traffic engineering standards convert vehicles into **Passenger Car Units (PCU)**:
- Two-Wheeler = `0.50 PCU`
- Auto-Rickshaw = `1.00 PCU`
- Passenger Car = `1.00 PCU`
- City Transit Bus = `3.00 PCU`
- Heavy Multi-Axle Truck = `3.50 PCU`

Our platform implements an **Indo-HCM (IRC:106) Dual-Criteria Evaluation Engine**. Rather than relying solely on speed or delay, the engine computes:

$$\text{LOS} = f(V/C, v_{\text{mean}})$$

If the $V/C$ ratio exceeds 0.85, the segment is automatically classified as LOS E or F regardless of momentary speed bursts, accurately reflecting the unstable queue state characteristic of Indian mixed arterial corridors.

### 2.3 Webster Minimum Delay Signal Timing Optimization

#### Mathematical Concept and Physical Derivation:
At a signalized urban intersection, vehicle delay occurs because cross-traffic must alternate right-of-way. In 1958, F.V. Webster modeled vehicular delay ($d$) at fixed-time isolated intersections using queueing theory:

$$d = \frac{C(1 - \lambda)^2}{2(1 - \lambda x)} + \frac{x^2}{2q(1 - x)} - 0.65\left(\frac{C}{q^2}\right)^{1/3} x^{(2 + 5\lambda)}$$

Where:
- $C$ is the cycle length in seconds.
- $\lambda = g / C$ is the proportion of the cycle that is effective green.
- $q$ is the arrival flow rate.
- $s$ is the saturation flow rate.
- $x = q / (\lambda s)$ is the degree of saturation.

By differentiating total intersection vehicular delay ($D_{\text{total}} = \sum q_i d_i$) with respect to cycle length $C$ and setting the partial derivative to zero:

$$\frac{\partial D_{\text{total}}}{\partial C} = 0$$

Webster derived the semi-empirical optimum cycle length formula that minimizes total vehicle delay:

$$C_0 = \frac{1.5 L + 5}{1 - Y}$$

Where:
- $L$ is the total lost time per cycle across all phases:
  $$L = \sum_{i=1}^{n} l_i = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (t_{\text{yellow}, i} + t_{\text{all-red}, i} - t_{\text{startup-delay}, i})$$
- $Y$ is the sum of the critical approach volume-to-saturation flow ratios:
  $$Y = \sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{q_i}{s_i}$$

Once optimum cycle length $C_0$ is determined, total effective green time is:

$$G_{\text{effective}} = C_0 - L$$

Effective green time is then allocated to each critical phase proportionally to its traffic demand ratio:

$$g_i = \left(\frac{y_i}{Y}\right) \cdot (C_0 - L)$$

#### Variable Definitions, Units, and Normal Ranges:
- `C_0`: Optimum Cycle Length in seconds (unit: seconds, normal practical range: `60s` to `180s`).
- `L`: Total Lost Time per cycle in seconds (unit: seconds, typical range: `10s` to `20s`).
- `Y`: Sum of Critical Approach Volume Ratios (dimensionless, normal range: `0.30` light to `0.85` heavily saturated).
- `q_i`: Critical approach vehicle arrival rate for phase $i$ (unit: veh/h, range: `200` to `1500 veh/h`).
- `s_i`: Approach saturation flow rate (unit: veh/h, typically `1800` to `2200 veh/h` per lane).
- `g_i`: Allocated effective green time for phase $i$ (unit: seconds).

#### Worked Numerical Example:
At the major corridor intersection Viman Nagar Chowk (`INT-VN-01`), we have a 2-phase signal plan:
- Total lost time per cycle: $L = 14.0\text{ seconds}$ ($2 \times 4.0\text{s yellow} + 2 \times 3.0\text{s all-red} - 2 \times 2.0\text{s startup allowance}$).
- Phase 1 (Arterial East-West Nagar Road): Critical arrival volume $q_1 = 920\text{ veh/h}$, saturation flow $s_1 = 2300\text{ veh/h}$.
  $$y_1 = \frac{920}{2300} = 0.400$$
- Phase 2 (Cross-Street North-South Viman Nagar): Critical arrival volume $q_2 = 630\text{ veh/h}$, saturation flow $s_2 = 1800\text{ veh/h}$.
  $$y_2 = \frac{630}{1800} = 0.350$$
- Sum of critical demand ratios:
  $$Y = y_1 + y_2 = 0.400 + 0.350 = 0.750$$

Step 1: Compute Optimum Cycle Length ($C_0$):
$$C_0 = \frac{1.5 \cdot 14.0 + 5.0}{1.0 - 0.750} = \frac{21.0 + 5.0}{0.250} = \frac{26.0}{0.250} = 104.0\text{ seconds}$$

Step 2: Compute Total Available Effective Green Time:
$$G_{\text{effective}} = C_0 - L = 104.0 - 14.0 = 90.0\text{ seconds}$$

Step 3: Allocate Proportional Green Splits:
- Phase 1 (Nagar Road Arterial):
  $$g_1 = \left(\frac{y_1}{Y}\right) \cdot G_{\text{effective}} = \left(\frac{0.400}{0.750}\right) \cdot 90.0 = 0.5333 \cdot 90.0 = 48.0\text{ seconds}$$
- Phase 2 (Viman Nagar Cross-Street):
  $$g_2 = \left(\frac{y_2}{Y}\right) \cdot G_{\text{effective}} = \left(\frac{0.350}{0.750}\right) \cdot 90.0 = 0.4667 \cdot 90.0 = 42.0\text{ seconds}$$

Step 4: Verification of Cycle Closure:
$$C_0 = g_1 + g_2 + L = 48.0 + 42.0 + 14.0 = 104.0\text{ seconds}$$
Exact cycle closure is verified. Total intersection delay is mathematically minimized.

### 2.4 Green Wave Arterial Coordination Progression Offset

#### Mathematical Concept and Physical Derivation:
When two signalized intersections are closely spaced on an arterial corridor, uncoordinated signals cause vehicles released from the upstream intersection to arrive during the red interval of the downstream signal.

To achieve continuous "Green Wave" platoon progression, the downstream signal must turn green at the exact moment the vehicle platoon arrives:

$$t_{\text{travel}} = \frac{D}{v_{\text{progression}} \cdot 0.2778}$$

Where:
- $D$ is the physical distance between stoplines in meters.
- $v_{\text{progression}}$ is the desired platoon progression speed in km/h.
- $0.2778$ is the conversion factor from km/h to meters per second ($1000 / 3600$).

Because traffic signal controllers operate cyclically, the coordination offset is computed using modulo arithmetic relative to the master cycle length ($C$):

$$t_{\text{offset}} = t_{\text{travel}} \pmod C$$

#### Worked Numerical Example:
- Corridor Distance between Viman Nagar Chowk (`INT-VN-01`) and Somnath Nagar Chowk (`INT-SN-01`):
  $$D = 1800\text{ meters}$$
- Platoon Target Progression Speed:
  $$v_{\text{progression}} = 40.0\text{ km/h}$$
- Velocity in meters per second:
  $$v_{\text{ms}} = 40.0 \cdot 0.2778 = 11.11\text{ m/s}$$
- Platoon travel time:
  $$t_{\text{travel}} = \frac{1800}{11.11} = 162.0\text{ seconds}$$
- Coordinated Master Cycle Length:
  $$C = 120.0\text{ seconds}$$
- Coordination Progression Offset:
  $$t_{\text{offset}} = 162.0 \pmod{120.0} = 42.0\text{ seconds}$$
- Operational interpretation: Somnath Nagar Chowk must schedule its arterial green phase to initiate exactly 42.0 seconds after Viman Nagar Chowk green initiation, allowing the vehicle platoon to pass without stopping.

### 3.1 Three-Phase Alternating Current (AC) Power Balance

#### Mathematical Concept and Physical Derivation:
In a balanced 3-phase alternating current electrical distribution system, the instantaneous voltages across the three phases ($A, B, C$) are separated by $120^\circ$ ($2\pi/3$ radians):

$$v_a(t) = \sqrt{2} V_{\text{LN}} \cos(\omega t)$$

$$v_b(t) = \sqrt{2} V_{\text{LN}} \cos\left(\omega t - \frac{2\pi}{3}\right)$$

$$v_c(t) = \sqrt{2} V_{\text{LN}} \cos\left(\omega t + \frac{2\pi}{3}\right)$$

For balanced loads with a phase displacement angle $\phi$ between voltage and current:

$$i_a(t) = \sqrt{2} I_L \cos(\omega t - \phi)$$

$$i_b(t) = \sqrt{2} I_L \cos\left(\omega t - \frac{2\pi}{3} - \phi\right)$$

$$i_c(t) = \sqrt{2} I_L \cos\left(\omega t + \frac{2\pi}{3} - \phi\right)$$

The total instantaneous power delivered to the 3-phase system is the sum of instantaneous power across all three phases:

$$p(t) = v_a(t) i_a(t) + v_b(t) i_b(t) + v_c(t) i_c(t)$$

Applying standard trigonometric identities:

$$\cos(\alpha)\cos(\beta) = \frac{1}{2}[\cos(\alpha - \beta) + \cos(\alpha + \beta)]$$

The time-varying double-frequency terms cancel out completely because:

$$\cos(2\omega t - \phi) + \cos\left(2\omega t - \frac{4\pi}{3} - \phi\right) + \cos\left(2\omega t + \frac{4\pi}{3} - \phi\right) = 0$$

Leaving constant, non-pulsating active electrical power:

$$P = 3 \cdot V_{\text{LN}} \cdot I_L \cdot \cos(\phi)$$

In 3-phase distribution systems, potential transformers measure the Line-to-Line voltage ($V_{\text{LL}}$), which relates to the Line-to-Neutral voltage by:

$$V_{\text{LL}} = \sqrt{3} \cdot V_{\text{LN}} \implies V_{\text{LN}} = \frac{V_{\text{LL}}}{\sqrt{3}}$$

Substituting $V_{\text{LN}}$ into the power equation yields the fundamental 3-phase active power equation:

$$P = 3 \cdot \left(\frac{V_{\text{LL}}}{\sqrt{3}}\right) \cdot I_L \cdot \cos(\phi) = \sqrt{3} \cdot V_{\text{LL}} \cdot I_L \cdot \cos(\phi)$$

Similarly, reactive power ($Q$), sustained by alternating magnetic fields in AC inductors and transformers, is:

$$Q = \sqrt{3} \cdot V_{\text{LL}} \cdot I_L \cdot \sin(\phi)$$

The total apparent power ($S$) represents the vector sum in the complex power plane:

$$S = \sqrt{P^2 + Q^2} = \sqrt{3} \cdot V_{\text{LL}} \cdot I_L$$

The electrical operating power factor ($\text{PF}$) is the ratio of real working power to apparent power:

$$\text{PF} = \frac{P}{S} = \cos(\phi)$$

#### Variable Definitions, Units, and Normal Ranges:
- `P`: Active Real Power doing physical thermodynamic work (unit: kilowatts, kW).
- `Q`: Reactive Power sustaining electromagnetic fields (unit: kilovolt-amperes reactive, kVAR).
- `S`: Apparent Power delivered through utility cables (unit: kilovolt-amperes, kVA).
- `V_LL`: Line-to-line RMS AC voltage (unit: Volts, normal: `415V` commercial distribution, `22000V` primary feeder).
- `I_L`: Line RMS AC current per phase (unit: Amperes, normal: `50A` to `4000A`).
- `PF`: Power Factor (dimensionless, normal utility target: `0.95` to `0.98`).

#### Worked Numerical Example: Phoenix Marketcity Retail Hub
At the facility main distribution switchgear:
- Line-to-line Voltage: $V_{\text{LL}} = 415.0\text{ Volts}$ ($0.415\text{ kV}$).
- Measured Phase Current: $I_L = 3600.0\text{ Amperes}$.
- Measured Power Factor: $\text{PF} = 0.960$ ($\cos\phi = 0.960$).
- Phase angle $\phi = \arccos(0.960) = 16.26^\circ$.
- Reactive factor $\sin\phi = \sin(16.26^\circ) = 0.280$.

Step 1: Compute Real Active Power ($P$):
$$P = \sqrt{3} \cdot V_{\text{LL}} \cdot I_L \cdot \cos(\phi) = 1.73205 \cdot 0.415\text{ kV} \cdot 3600.0\text{ A} \cdot 0.960 = 2484.2\text{ kW}$$

Step 2: Compute Total Apparent Power ($S$):
$$S = \sqrt{3} \cdot V_{\text{LL}} \cdot I_L = 1.73205 \cdot 0.415\text{ kV} \cdot 3600.0\text{ A} = 2587.7\text{ kVA}$$

Step 3: Compute Reactive Power ($Q$):
$$Q = \sqrt{3} \cdot V_{\text{LL}} \cdot I_L \cdot \sin(\phi) = 1.73205 \cdot 0.415\text{ kV} \cdot 3600.0\text{ A} \cdot 0.280 = 724.6\text{ kVAR}$$

Step 4: Vector Sum Verification:
$$\sqrt{P^2 + Q^2} = \sqrt{(2484.2)^2 + (724.6)^2} = \sqrt{6171249.6 + 525045.2} = \sqrt{6696294.8} = 2587.7\text{ kVA}$$
Exact vector match confirmed.

### 3.2 Kirchhoff Conservation of Energy in Corridor Microgrid

#### Mathematical Concept and Physical Derivation:
In an electrical distribution network, by Kirchhoff Current Law and the First Law of Thermodynamics, electrical energy cannot be created or destroyed. At every instant, the sum of power imported from the primary utility substation and local on-site distributed energy resources must equal total commercial power consumption plus technical line losses:

$$P_{\text{grid}} + P_{\text{solar}} + P_{\text{bess}} = P_{\text{loads}} + P_{\text{losses}}$$

Where:
- $P_{\text{grid}}$: Active power imported from the primary 22kV municipal grid (unit: kW).
- $P_{\text{solar}}$: Active power generated by rooftop photovoltaic solar arrays (unit: kW, non-negative).
- $P_{\text{bess}}$: Active power dispatched by Battery Energy Storage Systems (unit: kW, positive when discharging, negative when charging).
- $P_{\text{loads}}$: Aggregate real power consumed by commercial buildings, EV chargers, and street lighting (unit: kW).
- $P_{\text{losses}}$: Conductor ohmic heating ($I^2 R$) and transformer core hysteresis losses (typically 2.5% to 4.0%).

#### Worked Numerical Example:
At 14:00 PM during solar peak generation:
- Commercial Building Facility Demand: $P_{\text{loads}} = 4400.0\text{ kW}$.
- Distribution Line & Transformer Losses (3.0%): $P_{\text{losses}} = 132.0\text{ kW}$.
- Rooftop Solar PV Generation: $P_{\text{solar}} = 1200.0\text{ kW}$.
- BESS Battery Charging from Surplus Solar: $P_{\text{bess}} = -300.0\text{ kW}$ (charging).

Net Grid Import Required:
$$P_{\text{grid}} = P_{\text{loads}} + P_{\text{losses}} - P_{\text{solar}} - P_{\text{bess}}$$

$$P_{\text{grid}} = 4400.0 + 132.0 - 1200.0 - (-300.0) = 4532.0 - 1200.0 + 300.0 = 3632.0\text{ kW}$$

Verification:
$$3632.0\text{ (Grid)} + 1200.0\text{ (Solar)} - 300.0\text{ (Battery)} = 4532.0\text{ kW} = 4400.0\text{ (Load)} + 132.0\text{ (Losses)}$$
Kirchhoff energy balance is satisfied.

### 3.3 Feeder Thermal Loading and Electrical Headroom

#### Mathematical Concept and Physical Derivation:
Every underground distribution cable and substation step-down transformer has a physical thermal current rating ($I_{\text{rated}}$) determined by conductor cross-sectional area and insulation temperature limits (e.g. 90 deg C for XLPE cables). Continuous current beyond $I_{\text{rated}}$ accelerates insulation thermal degradation:

$$\Delta T \propto I^2 R$$

The percentage thermal ampacity utilization is:

$$\text{Utilization} = \left(\frac{I_{\text{measured}}}{I_{\text{rated}}}\right) \cdot 100\%$$

The safe remaining electrical power headroom before reaching rated ampacity is:

$$P_{\text{headroom}} = \sqrt{3} \cdot V_{\text{LL}} \cdot (I_{\text{rated}} - I_{\text{measured}}) \cdot \text{PF}$$

#### Worked Numerical Example:
On primary 22kV Distribution Feeder A ($V_{\text{LL}} = 22000.0\text{ Volts} = 22.0\text{ kV}$, $I_{\text{rated}} = 800.0\text{ Amperes}$, operating $\text{PF} = 0.970$):
- Current active telemetry reading: $I_{\text{measured}} = 612.0\text{ Amperes}$.

Step 1: Compute Feeder Thermal Utilization:
$$\text{Utilization} = \left(\frac{612.0}{800.0}\right) \cdot 100\% = 76.5\%$$
Status: Nominal operating condition (green status, below the 85.0% threshold).

Step 2: Compute Remaining Current Margin:
$$\Delta I = I_{\text{rated}} - I_{\text{measured}} = 800.0 - 612.0 = 188.0\text{ Amperes}$$

Step 3: Compute Available Active Electrical Headroom:
$$P_{\text{headroom}} = \sqrt{3} \cdot 22.0\text{ kV} \cdot 188.0\text{ A} \cdot 0.970 = 1.73205 \cdot 22.0 \cdot 188.0 \cdot 0.970 = 694.7\text{ kW}$$
Engineering interpretation: Commercial tenants on Feeder A can safely draw an additional 694.7 kW of power before the feeder reaches thermal warning capacity.

## 4. Environmental Science and Air Quality Monitoring

### 4.1 Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) Air Quality Index (AQI)

#### Mathematical Concept and Physical Derivation:
The Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB, Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change, India) defines the National Air Quality Index using segmented linear piecewise interpolation between epidemiological health-risk concentration breakpoints:

$$I = I_{\text{low}} + \left(\frac{I_{\text{high}} - I_{\text{low}}}{C_{\text{high}} - C_{\text{low}}}\right) \cdot (C - C_{\text{low}})$$

Where:
- $C$ is the monitored 24-hour average concentration of the specific pollutant.
- $C_{\text{low}}$ and $C_{\text{high}}$ are the lower and upper concentration breakpoints of the category containing $C$.
- $I_{\text{low}}$ and $I_{\text{high}}$ are the corresponding lower and upper AQI sub-index category values.

The composite city AQI is the maximum sub-index across all monitored criteria pollutants:

$$\text{AQI}_{\text{composite}} = \max(I_{\text{PM2.5}}, I_{\text{PM10}}, I_{\text{NO2}}, I_{\text{CO}}, I_{\text{SO2}})$$

#### Official CPCB Breakpoint Reference Table:

| AQI Category | Sub-Index Range | PM2.5 (ug/m3) | PM10 (ug/m3) | NO2 (ug/m3) | CO (mg/m3) |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **Good** | 0 to 50 | 0.0 to 30.0 | 0 to 50 | 0 to 40 | 0.0 to 1.0 |
| **Satisfactory** | 51 to 100 | 30.1 to 60.0 | 51 to 100 | 41 to 80 | 1.1 to 2.0 |
| **Moderate** | 101 to 200 | 60.1 to 90.0 | 101 to 250 | 81 to 180 | 2.1 to 10.0 |
| **Poor** | 201 to 300 | 90.1 to 120.0 | 251 to 350 | 181 to 280 | 10.1 to 17.0 |
| **Very Poor** | 301 to 400 | 120.1 to 250.0 | 351 to 430 | 281 to 400 | 17.1 to 34.0 |
| **Severe** | 401 to 500 | > 250.0 | > 430 | > 400 | > 34.0 |

#### Worked Numerical Example:
A CAAQMS station on the arterial corridor reports a 24-hour PM2.5 reading of $C = 48.5\text{ ug/m3}$:
- Reading falls in the **Satisfactory** category:
  - Concentration Breakpoints: $C_{\text{low}} = 30.1\text{ ug/m3}$, $C_{\text{high}} = 60.0\text{ ug/m3}$.
  - Sub-Index Breakpoints: $I_{\text{low}} = 51$, $I_{\text{high}} = 100$.

Compute Linear Interpolation:
$$I = 51 + \left(\frac{100 - 51}{60.0 - 30.1}\right) \cdot (48.5 - 30.1)$$

$$I = 51 + \left(\frac{49}{29.9}\right) \cdot 18.4 = 51 + (1.6388 \cdot 18.4) = 51 + 30.15 = 81.15 \to 81$$
Result: AQI = 81 (Satisfactory air quality). Minor breathing discomfort for sensitive individuals.

### 4.2 Statistical Anomaly Detection: Isolation Forest and Z-Score

#### Mathematical Concept and Physical Derivation:
To distinguish normal commuter rush emissions from sudden industrial discharges or sensor malfunctions, the digital twin runs an **Isolation Forest** anomaly detector.

An Isolation Forest operates on the principle that anomalies are "few and different". In a random tree partitioning feature space, anomalous points require fewer recursive random splits to become isolated in terminal leaf nodes.

For a dataset of $n$ instances, the average path length of an unsuccessful search in a Binary Search Tree (BST) represents the equivalent baseline:

$$c(n) = 2 \ln(n - 1) + 2\gamma - \frac{2(n - 1)}{n}$$

Where $\gamma \approx 0.5772156649$ is Euler-Mascheroni constant.

The anomaly score $s(x, n)$ of instance $x$ across an ensemble of $T$ isolation trees is:

$$s(x, n) = 2^{-\frac{E(h(x))}{c(n)}}$$

Where $E(h(x))$ is the expected path length (tree depth) across all trees.
- If $E(h(x)) \to 0 \implies s \to 1.0$: Instance isolated near the root; strong anomaly.
- If $E(h(x)) \to n - 1 \implies s \to 0.0$: Instance buried deep in tree; normal observation.
- If $E(h(x)) \to c(n) \implies s \to 0.5$: Average depth; typical cluster.

Concurrently, a parametric diurnal Z-score is computed against the time-of-day mean ($\mu$) and standard deviation ($\sigma$):

$$Z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$$

#### Worked Numerical Example:
At 09:00 AM, historical baseline for PM2.5 has mean $\mu = 45.0\text{ ug/m3}$ and $\sigma = 12.0\text{ ug/m3}$:
1. **Normal Observation ($x = 48.5\text{ ug/m3}$):**
   $$Z = \frac{48.5 - 45.0}{12.0} = \frac{3.5}{12.0} = +0.29$$
   Isolation Forest depth is normal ($s = 0.38 < 0.60$). Status: Normal traffic variation.
2. **Abnormal Surge ($x = 95.0\text{ ug/m3}$):**
   $$Z = \frac{95.0 - 45.0}{12.0} = \frac{50.0}{12.0} = +4.17$$
   $Z > 3.0$ and Isolation Forest score surges to $s = 0.74 \ge 0.60$.
   System flags an instant environmental anomaly on the operator console.

## 5. Machine Learning, Conformal Prediction, and Explainability

### 5.1 Multi-Horizon XGBoost Forecasting

The platform uses **XGBoost (Extreme Gradient Boosted Trees)** for multi-horizon (15m, 30m, 60m) speed and power forecasting.

#### Feature Matrix Construction:
To prevent artificial temporal discontinuities between 23:59 and 00:00, temporal features are mapped onto continuous trigonometric unit circles:

$$\sin_{\text{hour}} = \sin\left(\frac{2\pi \cdot t}{24}\right)$$

$$\cos_{\text{hour}} = \cos\left(\frac{2\pi \cdot t}{24}\right)$$

The feature vector $\mathbf{x}_t$ includes:
1. Autoregressive lags: $y_{t-15}$, $y_{t-30}$, $y_{t-45}$, $y_{t-60}$.
2. Rolling window statistics: $\mu_{60m}$, $\sigma_{60m}$, $\Delta_{15m}$.
3. Cyclical diurnal features: $\sin_{\text{hour}}$, $\cos_{\text{hour}}$, day of week.
4. Cross-domain spatial features: upstream intersection queue length and commercial power demand.

### 5.2 Conformal Prediction: Distribution-Free Uncertainty Quantification

#### Mathematical Concept and Physical Derivation:
Standard AI forecasting models emit only a point prediction ($\hat{y}$), or assume residuals follow a Gaussian normal distribution ($[\hat{y} - 1.96\sigma, \hat{y} + 1.96\sigma]$). In urban municipal systems, non-linear traffic shocks (e.g. accidents, monsoon rain) cause heavily skewed, non-Gaussian error distributions.

Our platform uses **Split Conformal Prediction** (Vovk et al.). Given an exchangeable calibration dataset of $n$ chronological samples $(X_1, Y_1), \dots, (X_n, Y_n)$ independent of model training:

Step 1: Compute non-conformity residuals on the calibration set:
$$R_i = |Y_i - \hat{f}(X_i)|, \quad i = 1, \dots, n$$

Step 2: For a target significance level $\alpha$ (e.g. $\alpha = 0.10$ for 90% confidence), determine the empirical quantile of the residuals:
$$\hat{q}_{1-\alpha} = \text{Quantile}\left(\frac{\lceil (n + 1)(1 - \alpha) \rceil}{n}, \{R_1, \dots, R_n\}\right)$$

Step 3: Construct the prediction interval for a new test instance $X_{n+1}$:
$$\hat{C}(X_{n+1}) = [\hat{f}(X_{n+1}) - \hat{q}_{1-\alpha}, \; \hat{f}(X_{n+1}) + \hat{q}_{1-\alpha}]$$

This mathematical construction guarantees finite-sample coverage validity without making any distributional assumptions:

$$P\left(Y_{n+1} \in \hat{C}(X_{n+1})\right) \ge 1 - \alpha$$

#### Worked Numerical Example:
- Point prediction at $t+30\text{m}$: $\hat{y} = 28.0\text{ km/h}$.
- Calibrated 90% Conformal residual quantile: $\hat{q}_{0.90} = 4.2\text{ km/h}$.
- 90% Conformal Prediction Interval:
  $$[28.0 - 4.2, 28.0 + 4.2] = [23.8\text{ km/h}, 32.2\text{ km/h}]$$
- Operational interpretation: City engineers are mathematically assured with 90% confidence that actual arterial speed in 30 minutes will fall between 23.8 and 32.2 km/h.

### 5.3 Explainable AI with TreeSHAP

#### Mathematical Concept and Physical Derivation:
To eliminate "black-box" resistance from municipal officials, our platform integrates **TreeSHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)**, rooted in cooperative game theory.

For an ensemble model $f(\mathbf{x})$, the Shapley value $\phi_i$ represents the unique fair marginal contribution of feature $i$ across all possible feature subsets $S \subseteq F \setminus \{i\}$:

$$\phi_i = \sum_{S \subseteq F \setminus \{i\}} \frac{|S|! (|F| - |S| - 1)!}{|F|!} \cdot \left[f(S \cup \{i\}) - f(S)\right]$$

The model prediction decomposes additively into the base expectation plus individual feature contributions:

$$f(\mathbf{x}) = \phi_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{M} \phi_i$$

#### Worked Numerical Example: Top-5 Feature Attribution Breakdown
- Base Expected Corridor Speed ($\phi_0$): $38.0\text{ km/h}$.
- Feature 1: Recent 15-minute speed trend ($\phi_1$): $-4.2\text{ km/h}$.
- Feature 2: Evening rush-hour diurnal multiplier ($\phi_2$): $-3.8\text{ km/h}$.
- Feature 3: Upstream intersection queue length ($\phi_3$): $-1.5\text{ km/h}$.
- Feature 4: Day of week ($ \phi_4$): $+0.8\text{ km/h}$.
- Final Model Forecast:
  $$f(\mathbf{x}) = 38.0 - 4.2 - 3.8 - 1.5 + 0.8 = 29.3\text{ km/h}$$
The operator screen displays these exact contributions in Recharts waterfall cards.

### 5.4 Data Drift Monitoring: Population Stability Index (PSI)

#### Mathematical Concept and Physical Derivation:
Over weeks and months, urban traffic and energy usage distributions drift due to seasonal changes, roadwork, or shifts in commuter transit choices. The platform continuously monitors model input distributions against baseline training distributions using the **Population Stability Index (PSI)**:

$$\text{PSI} = \sum_{b=1}^{B} (P_b - Q_b) \cdot \ln\left(\frac{P_b}{Q_b}\right)$$

Where:
- $B$ is the number of distribution bins (typically $B = 10$ deciles).
- $P_b$ is the proportion of actual observations falling in bin $b$.
- $Q_b$ is the expected proportion from the reference training baseline falling in bin $b$.

#### Interpretation Thresholds:
- $\text{PSI} < 0.10$: **STABLE** (No significant distribution shift; models remain accurate).
- $0.10 \le \text{PSI} < 0.25$: **WARNING** (Moderate drift; automated retraining pipeline queued).
- $\text{PSI} \ge 0.25$: **DRIFT** (Significant environmental shift; operator alerted to recalibrate).
